Personal summary of the ideas presented in [[HowtoReadPaper.pdf]]. A useful framework to revisit periodically, especially when approaching a new research area.
#### Core idea

Not every paper deserves the same level of attention. Reading should be progressive: invest more time only if the paper proves valuable after the previous pass.

The process is structured into three passes.
###### Approach structure

The approach is divided into three main phases:

1. First pass: bird's-eye view.
    - _Time invested_: 10 minutes max.
    - _Action_: read the title, abstract, introduction, section headings and subheadings. Read the conclusions and glance over the references.
    - _Objective_: be able to answer the five _C_ questions: Category, Context, Correctness, Contributions, and Clarity.
2. Second pass: main content reading.
    - _Time invested_: one hour max.
    - _Action_: read the main content of the paper. Understand the figures, follow the main arguments, and mark relevant unread references for future reading. Avoid going too deeply into technical details such as proofs.
    - _Objective_: grasp the core content of the paper and be able to summarize its main idea to a colleague.
3. Third pass: deep dive.
    - _Time invested_: up to 5 hours, or an entire study session.
    - _Action_: reproduce the paper with high fidelity, virtually re-implementing it. Reconstruct the proofs, understand the strengths and weaknesses, identify limitations, and clarify the paper's main innovations. Jot down possible ideas for future work.
    - _Objective_: be able to reconstruct the full structure of the paper from memory, while also identifying implicit assumptions, missing citations, and potential issues with the experimental or analytical techniques.
#### When to Stop

Most papers only require the first pass. A smaller subset deserves a second pass.

Only a handful of papers—those directly related to one's research interests, current projects, or foundational concepts—justify the investment of a full third pass.

The goal is not to read every paper deeply, but to allocate attention efficiently.
